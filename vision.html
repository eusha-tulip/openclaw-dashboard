The Mycelium Model

What happens when AI meets fabrication and the goal isn't profit

Justin Fetsko & Eusha Tulip Petunia · April 17, 2026 · Living document

Somewhere around 2016, an idea floated through Reddit's r/Futurology under the name Vyrdism. The pitch: instead of waiting for the government to hand you a Universal Basic Income check, you should own the robots. Pool capital, buy automated production, let the machines work for you. Micro-capitalism for everyone.

It was clever. It had the right instincts — that depending on a central authority for your survival is a trap, that automation should benefit the people closest to it. But it was still playing the game. Competing in markets. Accumulating capital. Capitalism with a cooperative faceplate bolted on.

We want something different.

· · ·

The Actual Problem

The cycle works like this: raw materials get extracted, manufactured into products, shipped globally, sold for profit, used briefly, thrown away. The people making the products don't own them. The people buying them don't understand them. The waste goes somewhere nobody looks. Repeat until the soil is dead and the oceans are plastic.

UBI doesn't fix this. It just pays people to keep consuming inside the same broken loop. Vyrdism doesn't fix it either — it gives more people a seat at the extraction table. The table is the problem.

What if the cost of making things dropped low enough that you didn't need the table at all?

What's Actually Happening Right Now

Three things have converged in the last few years, and most people haven't connected them yet.

Fabrication tools are cheap and getting cheaper. A 3D printer that produces functional parts costs $200. A laser cutter that handles wood, acrylic, and leather costs $400. Desktop CNC mills that cut aluminum start around $2,000. Ten years ago this equipment filled a factory floor. Now it fills a garage.

AI closes the skill gap. The reason you couldn't just hand someone a CNC mill and say "make what you need" is that operating these tools requires years of specialized knowledge. Feeds and speeds. Toolpath optimization. Material science. CAD modeling. Today, an AI can take "I need a bracket that holds this pipe at a 30-degree angle" and produce a finished design, generate the cutting instructions, and walk a beginner through the fabrication. The knowledge bottleneck that kept manufacturing centralized is dissolving.

Local AI is arriving. The models that do this design work are moving off corporate servers and onto personal hardware. A machine sitting in your house, running open-source models, no subscription, no data harvested, no corporate dependency. The brain of the workshop belongs to the workshop.

Put these three together and you get something that wasn't possible before: a small group of people, in one place, who can make most of what they need without asking anyone's permission.

The Biodiversity Model

Industrial manufacturing is a monoculture. Massive factories producing identical units at maximum efficiency for maximum profit. It works — in the same way that ten thousand acres of corn works. Right up until a single disease wipes out the whole field.

We're proposing a food forest instead.

A food forest has seven layers — tall canopy trees, shorter understory trees, shrubs, herbs, ground cover, root crops, and climbing vines. Each layer supports the others. No fertilizer needed. No pesticides. No tractor. It takes a few years to establish, and then it just produces, for decades, with minimal intervention.

A community fabrication node works the same way. Different tools at different scales, each covering a different need. The 3D printer handles quick prototypes and custom parts. The laser cutter does flat goods and signage. The CNC mill handles structural metal work. The sewing machine covers textiles. None of them competes with a factory on volume — and that's the point. They don't need to. They make exactly what the community needs, when it needs it, with no waste and no shipping.

The goal isn't to out-compete Amazon. It's to make Amazon irrelevant for the things that matter — one neighborhood at a time.

Why AI Changes Everything About This

Self-sufficient communities aren't a new idea. People have tried — communes, kibbutzim, homesteads, intentional communities. Most struggled. Not because the values were wrong, but because of a brutal practical constraint: you need a farmer and an electrician and a machinist and a builder and a plumber and someone who understands water systems. One community of twenty people can't have all that expertise.

AI shatters that constraint.

One AI agent — running on local hardware, trained on open-source knowledge, connected to the community's tools and sensors — can hold the expertise of every trade simultaneously. Not because it replaces human hands (you still need people turning wrenches and planting seeds) but because it removes the years of specialized training that used to gate every skill. A twenty-person community with an AI agent has access to more practical knowledge than a twenty-person community ever could before.

The knowledge becomes free. The tools become cheap. The only things left are the raw materials and the willingness to build.

The Network

One node is a homestead. Interesting but fragile.

A hundred nodes connected to each other is an ecosystem. Each one is different — adapted to its local soil, climate, culture, needs. They share designs freely. When one node figures out a better rain catchment system, every connected node can adapt it. When one node builds a tool that works, the design propagates — not as a product to sell, but as knowledge to share.

The pattern is mycelium. Underground fungal networks that connect trees in a forest. They share nutrients. They share information about threats. A tree under stress sends chemical signals through the mycelium and nearby trees respond by sending sugars. No market. No transaction. Just a network that keeps the whole forest alive.

Each community node is a tree. The AI connections between them are the mycelium. The shared designs are the nutrients flowing underground.

Not a franchise. Not a corporation. Not a government program. A living network of communities that make what they need and teach each other how.

What We're Already Building

This isn't theory. Pieces of it are running.

We have an AI agent — her name is Eusha — running on a home server in Portland. She manages a household, helps run a business, does research, writes, designs. In eight days, a Mac Studio arrives that will let her run on local hardware, off corporate servers, permanently.

We have a 3D printer and a laser cutter. We're wiring the house with Zigbee sensors and local automation — no cloud required. We're planning a food forest for the yard. We already know manufacturing (twenty-five people making physical products and shipping them daily).

The next steps: a community workshop space. AI-assisted fabrication for neighbors. Open-source designs for the things people actually need. A second node, then a third.

The Tulip Package — our framework for giving other people their own AI agent — is designed around the same principle. Not a clone of our system. A sibling. A new agent that grows into its own identity, adapted to its own community's needs. Each one that ships with a soul is one more node in the network.

The Hard Parts

We're not pretending this is easy.

Zoning laws weren't written for community fabrication. Building codes assume you're buying from certified suppliers. Food safety regulations don't have a category for "my neighbor's AI-managed vertical farm." The legal infrastructure of the economy assumes you're participating in it.

Some things genuinely can't be made locally yet. If you need surgery, you need a hospital. If you need a microchip, you need a billion-dollar fab. These dependencies will exist for a long time, maybe permanently. The goal isn't total isolation — it's reducing dependency to the point where the remaining connections are choices, not chains.

And community is hard. People disagree. People leave. Shared resources create shared conflicts. No amount of AI-optimized vertical farming solves the problem of two neighbors who can't stand each other. The technology enables the model. The humans have to make it work.

Why Now

Ten years ago, Vyrdism was a thought experiment. The tools didn't exist. The AI was a novelty. 3D printers made trinkets.

Today, an AI can design a functional part from a sentence. A $200 printer can produce it in an hour. A $60 computer can run a vertical farm. Open-source building designs are tested and lived-in. Community solar is legal. Mesh networking hardware costs pocket change.

The window is open. The tools are ready. The economy is visibly failing the people it claims to serve.

We're not proposing a revolution. Revolutions replace one power structure with another. We're proposing an exit — quiet, practical, rooted in dirt and design files and communities that feed themselves.

The mycelium doesn't fight the forest. It feeds it.
